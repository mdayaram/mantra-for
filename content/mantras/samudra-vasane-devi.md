## How it helps

The first thing most of us do each day is put our weight on the Earth without a thought. This verse turns that moment into an act of attention. Before standing, you address the ground as a living presence — a goddess robed in oceans, with mountains for her breast, the consort of Viṣṇu who sustains the world — and you ask her pardon for stepping on her.

Asking forgiveness for something unavoidable is the point. You cannot live without standing on the Earth, eating from her, and taking from her; the prayer doesn't pretend otherwise. It simply refuses to let that dependence go unnoticed. Starting the day with a small, sincere apology and thanks sets a tone of humility and gratitude that tends to carry: the floor, the food, the air become things received rather than taken for granted.

There is a quiet wordplay in the last line. *Kṣamasva*, "forgive," comes from the same root as *kṣamā*, one of the Earth's own names — "the patient one," she who bears everything. You are asking the one whose nature is forbearance to bear with you once more.

## How to practice

- Recite it on waking, sitting on the edge of the bed, before your feet touch the floor. It is often said right after *karāgre vasate lakṣmīḥ*, the verse for looking at your palms on waking.
- As you finish, touch the ground with your right hand and then touch your fingers to your eyes or forehead — a common way of offering the salutation.
- Bharatanatyam dancers recite it in the *bhūmi praṇām* that opens practice or performance, before striking the ground with their feet.
- It also fits other first steps: onto new land, into a garden, or at the start of a walk or a hike.

## Notes

Every epithet is a vocative, all addressed to the Earth: *samudra-vasanā* ("she whose garment is the ocean") and *parvata-stana-maṇḍalā* ("she whose round breasts are the mountains") are bahuvrīhi compounds, descriptions that become names. *Kṣamasva* is the second-person imperative (ātmanepada) of √*kṣam*, "to forgive, to endure"; *me* is the enclitic "my", so the last line is literally "forgive my foot-touch."

Common variants read *parvatastanamaṇḍite* ("adorned with mountain-breasts") for *-maṇḍale*, and the last word is often printed run together as *kṣamasvame*. Some recite *parvata-sthāna* ("whose seat is the mountains"), though *stana* is the usual reading.

Online sources attribute the verse variously to the Atharva Veda, the Purāṇas, or the dance manual *Abhinaya Darpaṇa*, but none cites a specific passage; treat its origin as unknown.
