## How it helps

The Chālīsā begins with an admission most frightened people will recognise: *buddhihīna tanu jānike* — "knowing myself to be without understanding." It doesn't pretend to be strong. It asks plainly for what's missing — *bala buddhi bidyā dehu mohiṃ*, "give me strength, intelligence, and knowledge" — and to have the afflictions that cloud the mind (*kalesa bikāra*) taken away.

Then, for forty verses, it holds the mind on someone who is everything fear is not. Hanumān leaps an ocean, burns a fortress, carries a mountain to save a dying friend, and does it all not for himself but in service of Rāma. Reciting his deeds is a way of borrowing that steadiness: the hymn turns attention from the size of the threat to the size of the help. It says outright that "with you as protector, there is no one to fear" (*tuma racchaka kāhū ko ḍara nā*), that whatever is hard in the world becomes easy by his grace, and that "troubles are cut away and all pain ends" for whoever remembers him. For many people it is the thing they say under their breath in a hospital corridor, on a dark road, before an exam, or awake at night with a racing heart.

The rhythm helps as much as the words. Forty rhyming couplets in a steady, familiar metre give a frightened mind a track to run on — something to do with the breath and the voice for six or seven minutes, long enough for the body to settle. And because Hanumān's strength is always in service, the hymn reframes courage itself: not the absence of fear, but being held by something larger and acting anyway.

## How to practice

- Recite it once through, morning or evening; Tuesdays and Saturdays are traditionally Hanumān's days.
- Many people learn it by heart and say it in moments of fear or danger — when travelling, before surgery, or when a trouble seems too big to face.
- Sing along with the recording; it takes about seven minutes. The opening two dohās can be repeated on their own as a short prayer for strength and clear thinking.
- Verse 38 speaks of reciting it a hundred times; some devotees do 7, 11, or 108 recitations as a vow (*anuṣṭhāna*), but a single attentive recitation is the ordinary practice.

## Notes

The *Hanumān Chālīsā* ("the forty on Hanumān") is in Awadhi, an eastern Hindi language, not Sanskrit: two opening dohās, forty chaupāīs (rhyming couplets of sixteen-mātrā half-lines), and a closing dohā. It is traditionally attributed to Tulsīdās, author of the *Rāmcaritmānas*, and verse 40 names him; the attribution is near-universal in practice but has been questioned, partly because the hymn was absent from early printed editions of his collected works and no commentary on it was written before the 1980s. It is a separate work, not part of the *Rāmcaritmānas*.

The text here follows the common devotional reading (as in the sanskritdocuments.org edition), which is also what the recording sings. Jagadguru Rambhadracharya's critical *Mahāvīrī* commentary (1983) argues for several different readings, which some reciters follow:

- v. 6 *śaṅkara svayaṃ kesarīnandana* ("Śiva himself, the son of Kesarī") for *śaṅkara suvana* ("son of Śiva") — Hanumān as Śiva's incarnation rather than his son.
- v. 27 *saba para rāma rāya siratājā* ("King Rāma, crown over all") for *saba para rāma tapasvī rājā*.
- v. 28 *tāsu amita jīvana phala pāvai* for *soī amita…*.
- v. 32 *sādara ho raghupati ke dāsā* ("you are, with reverence, the servant of Rāma") for *sadā raho…*.
- Smaller differences: *bala budhi* for *bala buddhi*, *chājai* for *sājai* (v. 5), *lahahiṃ* for *lahai* (v. 22).

Spelling is Awadhi, which often writes *b* for Sanskrit *v* (*bidyā*, *bikrama*) and *s* for *ś* (*kalesa*, *kapīsa*). The transliteration writes every inherent *a*; in singing most word-final *a*s are silent (*carana* is sung *caran*). Candrabindu (nasal vowel) is written *m̐*.

"Śrī guru" in the first dohā is read by Rambhadracharya as Śiva, Hanumān's guru; it is more often taken as one's own teacher. The *phala cāri* ("four fruits") are the four aims of life — dharma, artha, kāma, and mokṣa. Verse 18 refers to the story of the infant Hanumān leaping at the rising sun, mistaking it for a ripe fruit.

Many recordings add a closing *āratī* (*maṅgala mūrati māruta nandana…*) or the Rāma dhvani *siyāvara rāmacandra kī jaya*; neither is part of the Chālīsā proper.
